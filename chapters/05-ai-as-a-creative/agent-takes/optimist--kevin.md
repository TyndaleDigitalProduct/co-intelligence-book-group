# Optimist's take — Chapter 5: AI as a Creative

## The possibilities worth taking seriously

There's a point in this chapter Mollick sets up and then kind of skips past.
On p. 105 he mentions a pattern from creativity research: people who come up
with great ideas mostly just come up with a lot of ideas. The odds of any one
idea being a winner stay about the same, so more tries means more winners.
Then on p. 104 there's the Wharton contest, where GPT-4's product ideas beat
the students' ideas and it spit them out way faster.

Put those together and it's pretty simple. If coming up with ideas gets cheap,
the hard part becomes picking the good ones. That's good news for us, because
knowing what's good is basically what a publisher does all day. We've got
editors whose whole job is judgment.

The other big one for me is who gets the most help. On p. 110, in the writing
study, the weaker writers improved the most and the gap between people shrank.
P. 105 says the most creative people get the least out of it, and people
usually quote that as a knock on AI. I read it the other way. It means the
people who were furthest behind got the biggest boost. In publishing, that's
somebody with a great idea who doesn't write well yet. Now they can get a
draft in good enough shape that an editor will actually read it.

And p. 113 surprised me. People rated ChatGPT's answers to patient questions
as more caring than the doctors' answers. I'm not saying the AI cares about
anybody. But a lot of what feels like caring is just somebody taking their
time with you, and doctors don't have time. If people get a first stop that
never rushes them, more of them are going to feel listened to.

## Where the book was too cautious

On p. 98 he talks about letting AI go back and fix its own mistakes like it's
a someday idea from a research paper. It showed up about a year later. Now a
lot of models check their own work before they answer, and having AI pull from
real sources instead of making things up is a normal setting you can turn on.
The thing he was most careful about predicting got here the fastest.

He also says making stuff up is just the price you pay for creativity (pp.
98-99), like you can't have one without the other. Honestly, that's not how it
works day to day. When I need facts, I point it at sources. When I want wild
ideas, I let it run. I switch back and forth all the time without really
thinking about it. It's less of a tradeoff and more like picking the right
setting for the job.

And The Button on p. 119, the one that writes the whole thing for you, always
gets talked about as a trap. But back on p. 117 there's an Adobe number saying
only 31 percent of people feel like they're living up to their creative potential.
The Button's only a problem if you use it on work that actually matters. Use
it on the busywork and it frees up time for the stuff you care about.

## What could make this real (and for whom)

P. 122 is the part I'd want us to spend the most time on. He talks about work
that exists mostly to look thorough, not because anyone uses it. Publishing
has plenty of that: reports and forms that get filled out because they always
have been. Once AI can knock one of those out in seconds, it gets real obvious
real fast whether anybody was reading it. That's a good excuse to stop doing
the stuff nobody reads and put that time into the work it was supposed to
support.

The people who benefit most are the ones who've never been able to say "does
anyone even read this?" without it sounding like complaining. Now the AI kind
of says it for them.

## The invitation to the group

Try the "lots of ideas" thing for real, once, on something that matters. Next
time you'd normally bring three options to a decision, have AI help you bring
forty. Then spend the time you saved picking the best one instead of coming up
with more. Come back and tell us if the best of forty beat the best of three.
Girotra's follow-up on p. 108 makes the point: what counts is how good your
*best* idea is, not how good your ideas are on average. I'd be bummed if
nobody tried it.
