# AI as a Creative: notes from Kevin

_Read on: 2026-09-23 · From 21 highlighted passages, pp. 93–122_

## What stuck with me

The recommendation letter on p. 121. Some writing matters because somebody
took the time, not because of what it says. A professor spending an evening
on your letter is the whole message. AI keeps the words and drops the time, so
the words stop meaning much. And then the twist: the AI letter "is going to be
better than most letters of recommendation" he gets, so refusing to use it
might actually hurt the student. I hadn't put that into words before, but it
applies to a lot more than letters. Thank-you notes, a personal reply to
someone who's upset, anything where the effort is the point.

The first-draft problem on p. 120. Writing is how you figure out what you
think, and if the AI does the first draft you can skip the figuring. The MIT
study backs it up: ChatGPT "mostly serves as a substitute for human effort,
not a complement," and most people didn't even edit what it gave them. That
one hit close, because I have AI draft a lot of my stuff. My fix is to decide
what the thing needs to say before I look at the draft, not after.

"Same-y" on p. 105. Any single AI idea can beat most people's (35 of the top
40 in the Wharton contest were ChatGPT's), but a room full of people still
covers more ground. I've seen this for real. I have a standing note that our
app review replies kept landing on "made our day." Each one looked fine alone.
Read ten in a row and it was obvious.

## Where I pushed back

**He says the hallucinating and the creativity come as a package** (p. 99).
It's one system, sure, but day to day you can steer it. Give it sources when
you need facts, let it run when you want ideas. It's less "you can't have one
without the other" and more "pick which one you want for this job."

**"Outsource some of the most difficult aspects of creativity"** (pp.
109–110). He hands the AI the coming-up-with-ideas part and keeps the picking
for people. I'm not sure coming up with ideas is the hard part. Picking the
good one out of a pile of forty takes knowing your audience and your goal, and
a bigger pile makes that harder, not easier. Also, his students' ideas getting
better is one professor's impression, not a study.

**"We have done it before, many times"** (p. 122). The examples are real
(musicians moving to live shows, painters moving past realism after
photography, clerks after spreadsheets). But every one of those took years and
plenty of people didn't make it through. "We" got through it. A lot of
individuals didn't.

**Small one:** p. 117 says 31 percent of people feel they're living up to
their creative potential. The optimist take I ran on this chapter says "about
half," which doesn't match the book.

## Connections

**Chapter 4, p. 66.** I wondered if he'd come back to "ask it why and it just
makes up a reason." He does on p. 96: models aren't built to say "I don't
know," they give you a confident answer instead. Then on p. 97 he admits the
three paragraphs about the fake-cases lawyer were written by GPT-4 and were
"almost right." Best demo in the book. The big errors get caught, the small
ones slide right through.

**Chapter 2, the pirate.** The George Washington time machine trick on p. 115
(ChatGPT won't write a Washington interview until you tell it he had a time
machine) is the same move as the pirate getting it to explain napalm. Give it a
story and it'll go along. Harmless here, same trick either way.

**Chapter 2, permission.** The toothbrush drummer on p. 102 (an AI idea that
matches a 1965 photo nobody can say was or wasn't in the training data) is the
copyright question from the other side. Not "did they train on our stuff" but
"is our AI-assisted work quietly borrowing someone else's."

**Work.** p. 115 might be the most encouraging page in the book for a
publisher. The people who get the best results are writers and editors,
because the skills are normal editing skills: describe the effect you want,
give specific feedback, know lots of examples. Nothing technical. And p. 96 is
the one I'd want in front of anyone setting up a support chatbot. One that
makes up a confident answer is worse than one that says "let me get a person."

## Questions I'm bringing

- If a message matters because someone spent time on it (p. 121), what happens
  to thank-you notes, personal replies, and reviews once everyone knows AI
  could have written them? Does that kind of signal just move somewhere else,
  like a phone call or showing up in person?
- p. 115 says writers and editors are the best prompters. If that's true, are
  our editors actually in the room when we try out these tools, or is it
  mostly the tech people?
- If newer people learn a job by editing AI drafts instead of writing from
  scratch (p. 120), do they ever build the judgment to know when a draft is
  wrong?
