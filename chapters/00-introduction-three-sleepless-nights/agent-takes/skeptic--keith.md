# Skeptic's take — Introduction: Three Sleepless Nights

*(This take doubles as the exemplar for the format — see `personas/skeptic.md`.)*

## The claims under attack

The introduction makes three load-bearing claims: (1) AI is a
**general-purpose technology** comparable to steam power or the internet;
(2) unlike those technologies, its impact is arriving **at unprecedented
speed**; and (3) the right response is **mandatory engagement** — Mollick
requires AI in his classes and urges us to invite it into everything we do.

## Where they break

**The speed claim smuggles in a measurement trick.** Mollick's evidence for
overnight transformation is capability demos and adoption headlines —
ChatGPT's record-setting user growth, an exam passed, an essay written. But
general-purpose technologies are defined by *diffusion through practice*, not
by sign-ups. Two-plus years after publication, the pattern looks more
classical than the introduction predicted: dramatic individual productivity
stories coexisting with organizations that have piloted much and
restructured little. That's the normal GPT curve Mollick says we're
skipping — which suggests we're not skipping it.

**Insomnia is doing argumentative work.** The sleepless-nights frame — awe
plus dread as evidence of significance — is exactly the emotional response
every hyped technology has produced in its early adopters, including ones
that mattered far less than promised. The intensity of a Wharton professor's
vertigo tells us about the demo, not the destination.

**"Mandatory engagement" generalizes poorly from a business school.**
Mollick's students are adults, evaluated on output, in a field where fluent
prose is the product. The introduction slides from "it worked in my
classroom" toward "everyone should do this" without pausing on the settings
where the ban instinct might be right — early skill formation, high-liability
work, or anywhere the human's unassisted judgment is the thing being trained.

## What I concede

The core empirical bet has aged well: the models did keep getting better, and
"assume this is the worst AI you will ever use" has been the single most
durable sentence in the book. And Mollick's insistence on *using* the systems
rather than theorizing about them is why the book still reads as honest where
more confident 2024 punditry now reads as embarrassing.

## The question for the group

If the transformation is real but arriving on a normal, institution-limited
timeline rather than overnight — what, concretely, does that change about the
advice in the rest of the book?
