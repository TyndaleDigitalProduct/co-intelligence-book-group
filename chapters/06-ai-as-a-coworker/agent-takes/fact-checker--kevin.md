# Fact-check: Chapter 6, AI as a Coworker

*The book came out in April 2024. I checked it against live sources on
2026-09-30. Most of the citations are already listed in this folder's
`sources.md`, and I added the newer ones under Related reading.*

## Scoreboard

| Claim | Verdict | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Well-paid, educated jobs overlap with AI the most. Telemarketer is #1 and professors fill most of the top 20 (p. 124) | **HELD UP** | That's what the Felten, Raj, and Seamans exposure ranking says: telemarketers first, then lots of college teaching jobs. [SSRN](https://ssrn.com/abstract=4414065) |
| BCG consultants with AI were faster and better, but worse on the task built to trip up the AI (pp. 127–128) | **HELD UP** | It's now peer-reviewed in *Organization Science* (2026). On the 18 tasks the AI could handle, people with it finished 12.2% more tasks and worked 25.1% faster. On the task outside the frontier, they were 19 points less likely to get it right. [HBS PDF](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/dell-acqua-et-al-2026-navigating-the-jagged-technological-frontier_5c589c8c-fbb5-458f-b285-c944746cd717.pdf) |
| There are two good ways to work with AI, Centaur and Cyborg (pp. 136–137) | **SUPERSEDED** | His own team took a closer look at 244 of the consultants and found a third type, **Self-Automators**, who just hand the whole task over. Centaurs got better at their field, Cyborgs got better with AI, and Self-Automators got better at neither. [HBS working paper](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=68273) |
| Recruiters with really good AI "fell asleep at the wheel" (p. 129) | **UNVERIFIED** | The source is a 2021 Columbia dissertation. I didn't find a public copy this time, so I couldn't check the numbers. |
| People hide their AI use at work. Over half in his Twitter poll said so (p. 144) | **HELD UP** | A much bigger survey says the same thing. Microsoft and LinkedIn asked 31,000 workers: 78% of AI users bring their own tools, and 52% don't want to admit using AI on their most important tasks. [Work Trend Index 2024](https://news.microsoft.com/source/2024/05/08/microsoft-and-linkedin-release-the-2024-work-trend-index-on-the-state-of-ai-at-work/) |
| Automated Tasks are "a very small category … for now," and agents act like confused interns (pp. 135–136) | **SUPERSEDED** | The "for now" ran out fast. METR measures how long a task AI agents can finish on their own. That length now doubles about every 4 months, up from every 7 months over the longer history. [METR](https://metr.org/blog/2026-1-29-time-horizon-1-1/) |
| It's unclear whether AI output is protected by copyright (p. 133) | **SUPERSEDED** | In the US this is mostly settled now. On March 2, 2026, the Supreme Court declined to take *Thaler v. Perlmutter*, so work made entirely by AI can't be copyrighted. How much a human has to add is still an open question. [Mayer Brown](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2026/03/supreme-court-denies-review-in-ai-authorship-case) |
| The weakest workers get the biggest boost (pp. 156–157) | **CONTESTED** | The call center study held up. It's now in the *QJE*: 14% better on average, 34% for new and low-skill agents. [QJE](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article/140/2/889/7990658) But a study of Kenyan business owners found the opposite. The strong ones gained about 15%, and the ones already struggling lost about 10%. [Management Science](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2024.06909) |
| AI will have very little effect on overall jobs in the next few years (p. 155) | **CONTESTED** | Overall, yes. Yale's Budget Lab found "no discernible disruption" in the job market as a whole. [Yale](https://budgetlab.yale.edu/research/evaluating-impact-ai-labor-market-current-state-affairs) Young workers are a different story. People aged 22 to 25 in AI-exposed jobs are now 19% below where they'd be otherwise. [Stanford](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/news/canariesaug26/) |
| Telephone operators: jobs overall adjusted, but experienced operators took the hit (pp. 155–156) | **HELD UP** | That's what Feigenbaum and Gross found (*QJE*, 2024). The chapter's endnotes don't cite them, though, and I couldn't confirm his "15 percent" figure. [QJE](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article-abstract/139/3/1879/7614605) |
| Most men (66%) and a quarter of women chose shocks over sitting quietly for 15 minutes (p. 152) | **HELD UP, small** | The real numbers are 12 of 18 men and 6 of 24 women (Wilson et al., *Science* 2014). That's a small group, and the study was about sitting alone with your thoughts, not about work. [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4330241/) The worm study is real, but I couldn't confirm the 18% vs. 2%. |

## The most consequential update since 2024

The Self-Automators. On p. 128 Mollick says most consultants "were simply
pasting in the questions." Later work from his own team shows that's not just
a quality problem, it's a learning problem. The people who handed everything
off didn't get any better at the work or at using AI.

Put that next to the Stanford data, where the jobs shrinking are entry-level
ones. Those are the people who'd normally learn the job by doing the boring
parts. So the chapter's "hand off the boring stuff" advice (p. 154) has a
cost it doesn't mention: the boring stuff was often the training.

## What this does to the chapter's argument

The main framework holds up fine. Jobs are bundles of tasks, the BCG results
survived peer review, and people really do hide their AI use.

Two things got weaker. First, "AI is a great leveler" is more like "AI levels
some tasks and widens the gap on others," and the difference seems to be
whether you can tell good advice from bad. Second, "not much change in jobs
for a few years" is true overall but not for people just starting out.

One more thing worth knowing. In 2025 MIT disowned a widely shared paper
showing AI helped *top* scientists most, because it couldn't vouch for the
data. [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/05/17/mit-disavows-doctoral-students-paper-on-ai-productivity-benefits)
The "who benefits" research moves fast, and not all of it holds up.

## The question for the group

If Self-Automators don't learn anything, and entry-level jobs are the ones
shrinking, who's going to be experienced enough in ten years to catch the AI
when it's wrong?
