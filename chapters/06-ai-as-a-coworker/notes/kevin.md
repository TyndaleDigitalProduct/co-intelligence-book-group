# AI as a Coworker: notes from Kevin

_Read on: 2026-09-30 · From 31 highlighted passages, pp. 123–158_

## What stuck with me

Falling asleep at the wheel (pp. 127–129). The BCG consultants with AI were
faster and better on basically everything, and it held up across 118
different checks. Then the catch: most of them just pasted in the question and
took the answer. On the one task built so the AI would get it wrong, people
without AI got it right 84 percent of the time and people with AI dropped to
60 to 70 percent. The recruiter study is even weirder. Recruiters given really
good AI did worse than ones given mediocre AI, because they stopped checking.
That's the chapter 5 first-draft worry again, but with numbers.

The workers find the uses, not the leaders (p. 145). He says flat out that
strategy councils and IT are "likely pretty bad at figuring out the best-use
cases for AI," and the people doing the job are "far more likely to find
powerful and targeted uses." But they hide it, because of bans, because AI
work lands better when people don't know it's AI, and because if you automate
90 percent of your job and tell your boss, maybe 90 percent of your coworkers
go. "Better not to speak up." His fix (p. 146) is that companies have to take
"fire people, save money" off the table, or nobody will share.

His three AI editors (pp. 138–141). Ozymandias tells him to cut, Mnemosyne
finds odd connections, and Steve is a regular reader who's confused about being
inside a computer. Steve's feedback "shaped the chapter." That's basically what
our persona setup in this repo is. I didn't realize it came straight out of the
book.

## Where I pushed back

**"Tasks that we are not good at, can be outsourced to AI"** (p. 154). Boring
tasks, sure. But handing off what you're bad at means you might never get
better at it. He even shows the risk himself two pages later: the weakest
workers get the biggest boost (pp. 156–157). Great for them today, but do
they ever build the skill, or just lean on the tool?

**Delegated Tasks you "will still check"** (p. 134). The framework assumes
the checking happens. But his own recruiter study says the better the AI gets,
the less people check. So the category he says is safe is the one that slides
into the risky one without anybody deciding it should.

**The boredom stats** (p. 152). Two-thirds of men picking electric shocks over
sitting alone for 15 minutes, and 18 percent of bored people killing worms.
Fun to read, but those are small studies about boredom in general, not
boredom at work. I'd want to look at them before using them to make the point
about jobs.

**Sending the summary without saying AI wrote it** (pp. 134–135). He emails
Dell'Acqua an AI summary of Dell'Acqua's own paper and doesn't mention the AI.
It worked out, but it's the p. 144 hiding problem from the author's own desk.
Not a huge deal, just funny.

## Connections

**Chapter 5, p. 120.** The MIT study comes back on p. 128: most people
"didn't even bother editing the AI's output." Chapter 5 said first drafts from
AI mean less thinking. This chapter says it also means worse results when the
AI is wrong.

**Chapter 5, p. 118.** Poor writers stop getting held back, now with more
studies behind it (p. 157): bad writers become solid, the least creative get
the biggest boost, the worst law student writers become good ones, and a call
center's weakest workers got 35 percent more productive.

**Chapter 5, p. 97, again.** On p. 132 he has the AI copy his style and write
the reasons an author shouldn't let AI write for them. It's good. Same trick
as the GPT-4 paragraphs about the lawyer: showing it while saying he doesn't
do it.

**Chapter 3, the rules.** "Invite AI to everything" is how he says to learn
where it's good and bad (p. 142), and "this is the worst AI you will ever use"
shows up on p. 131. The task categories only work if you keep checking where
the line is, because it keeps moving.

**Work.** The Featurebase AI assistant that answers support conversations is
close to what he calls an Automated Task, one nobody checks each time. By his
framework that's the category that needs the most trust, and today "AI makes
too many mistakes to use in an automated fashion" (p. 135). Also, a "Steve"
for help articles and release notes (someone who doesn't know the app reading
it first) is something I'd actually use.

## Questions I'm bringing

- How do you stay awake at the wheel when the AI is right almost every time?
  Spot checks, checklists, or does it just take knowing the answer before you
  look (pp. 128–129)?
- If the best uses come from the people doing the work (p. 145), is there
  anywhere here where people feel safe showing how they use AI? Or is most of
  it hidden, like his poll says?
- If AI helps the weakest workers the most (pp. 156–157), what happens to
  training? Do newer people ever build the skill, or does the AI become the
  skill?
- What's on each of our Just Me lists (pp. 130–133)? And is anything on there
  because we like doing it, not because it matters?
