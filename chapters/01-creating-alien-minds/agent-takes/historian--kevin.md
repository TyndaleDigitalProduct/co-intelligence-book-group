# Historian's take: Chapter 1, Creating Alien Minds

*Focus taken from the question pool item asking what the precedent is for
deploying a technology its makers cannot explain.*

## The chapter's implicit historical bets

Three bets. None is stated as history, all of them rest on it.

1. That we can responsibly use a technology whose builders cannot explain it
   (p. 26, and the tic-tac-toe demonstration on p. 25, where the model writes a
   working game in one pass and then misreads the board).
2. That the contested origins of the training corpus (p. 13) are weather to be
   waited out rather than a barrier.
3. That because capability arrived unpredictably, adoption will follow at the
   same speed. Hence "assume this is the worst AI you will ever use."

## Precedents that support them

Bet 1 is the defensible one, and the record is not close. Working steam engines
ran for a century before thermodynamics explained them: Newcomen in 1712, Watt
in the 1770s, Carnot's theory in 1824. Bayer sold aspirin from 1899; John Vane
established the mechanism in 1971 and took a Nobel for it in 1982. General
anesthesia has been routine since 1846 and its mechanism is still unsettled.
Opacity is the normal condition of a useful technology. Mollick is right that
"nobody knows why it works" is not on its own a reason to stand back.

Bet 2 has a strong precedent too, and this group should know it cold, because
publishing has run the play four times. In *White-Smith v. Apollo* (1908) the
Supreme Court held that piano rolls were not copies of sheet music. Publishers
lost. Congress answered inside a year, and the 1909 Act's compulsory mechanical
license replaced the lawsuit with a market. Photocopying repeated it:
*Williams & Wilkins* (1973), fair use codified in the 1976 Act, then the
Copyright Clearance Center in 1978. Betamax in 1984, then home video as a
revenue line. Napster enjoined in 2001, licensed streaming about a decade
later. The shape is stable. The technology deploys, the courts find the old
statute does not fit, and the durable settlement is a license rather than a
prohibition.

## Precedents that complicate them

Here the chapter's comfort is too cheap.

In every case where we used what we could not explain, the thing that made it
safe was not eventual understanding. It was an outside evaluation regime, and
it usually arrived after a body count. Boilers exploded on American steamboats
through the 1830s, and the Steamboat Acts of 1838 and 1852 imposed inspection.
The Grover Shoe Factory explosion of 1905 killed 58 people and produced the
ASME Boiler Code within a decade. Thalidomide produced the Kefauver-Harris
amendments of 1962, which is why a drug now has to prove efficacy rather than
merely avoid obvious harm. Comfort came from mandatory testing by parties with
no stake in the result. We have nothing like that here. The industry grades its
own homework, which is what the optimist take ran into when GPT-4's bar exam
percentile deflated under outside scrutiny.

On bet 3, electrification is the standing rebuke to any assumption that
capability implies adoption. Electric motors were commercially available in the
1880s. The American manufacturing productivity surge came in the 1920s, because
the gain required abandoning the central line shaft and redesigning the factory
floor around unit drive. Forty years. Solow's 1987 remark about seeing the computer age everywhere except
in the productivity statistics has the same shape, and that payoff showed up in
the late 1990s. Adoption is gated by organizations, law and norms. Chapter 1 is
a capability chapter, so it has nothing to say about the gate.

## Where history offers no guide

Two real disanalogies, worth naming rather than forcing.

Every prior general-purpose technology reached workers through their employer.
The factory bought the motor, the firm bought the mainframe. This one arrived
in a browser tab, so it diffused to individuals before institutions held any
position on it at all. That inverts the usual order, and it means our de facto
policy is whatever people here are already doing unsupervised.

And no earlier technology was wrong while sounding authoritative. A failed
boiler is unambiguous. That is the genuine novelty in the p. 25 demonstration,
and the reason the evaluation regime cannot be borrowed from an existing field.

## The question for the group

If opacity became tolerable historically only when a disinterested party built
the test, and nobody is building ours, what would a Tyndale-grade evaluation of
these tools actually look like, and who in this room would own it? The optimist
take proposes writing our own benchmark. History suggests that is not an
enrichment exercise on the side of the reading. It is the entire mechanism by
which this becomes safe to use.
