# Optimist's take — Chapter 3: Four Rules for Co-Intelligence

## The possibilities worth taking seriously

Start with the one Mollick states almost in passing: the Jagged Frontier (p.
47) means nobody, including the labs, actually knows the shape of the wall
in advance. Taken seriously, that's not a caveat, it's an opening. The only
way to find where the wall bends toward you is to push on it yourself, which
means the advantage no longer belongs to whoever has the biggest budget for
R&D. It belongs to whoever is willing to run the experiment.

That's exactly the argument the user-innovation research on p. 48 makes,
and it's worth taking further than the chapter does. Von Hippel's whole body
of work says the best ideas come from the people closest to the problem, not
from a lab, because they're the ones who feel where the shoe pinches. What's
new is the cost of testing an idea against a genuinely capable collaborator
just fell to nearly zero. Someone with a real problem and no budget now has
the same first move a well-funded team has: try it and see. That's a
meaningful redistribution of who gets to innovate, not a marginal one.

The calculator argument (p. 51) deserves the same treatment. Every
capability panic about a new tool, the calculator, spell-check, GPS, ended
with people who used the tool deliberately getting measurably better at the
underlying skill, not worse. If that pattern holds here, and the book bets
that it does, the honest prediction isn't "people get lazier," it's "the
floor under everyone's reasoning rises," because they now have something
that reliably flags their own blind spots.

## Where the book was too cautious

Mollick was writing in 2024, and two of his own citations have aged into
better news than he had room to say. The hallucination discussion (p. 53)
treats "good at justifying a wrong answer" as a durable structural problem.
It's real, but the trend line since has been consistently downward, and
grounding a model's answers in retrieved sources rather than memory alone
has gone from a research idea to a default feature in the tools most people
actually use. Some of the caution he built in has already dated well in the
right direction.

The persona-prompting research on p. 59 is the clearest case. He cites one
HBS working paper showing that assigning a model different personas
surfaces meaningfully different, useful perspectives. That finding is no
longer a curiosity, it's become a whole category: on-demand advisory boards,
simulated focus groups, structured multi-perspective debate, all built on
exactly the mechanism he found in a single footnote. He undersold how fast
that would turn into infrastructure.

## What could make this real (and for whom)

The clearest beneficiary is anyone currently priced out of expertise, not
by ability but by access: a second opinion, a domain review, a sharper
editor. "Treat it as a coeditor, not a machine you prompt once" (~p. 63-64)
is the whole method, and it doesn't require credentials to use well, just
the willingness to go back and forth instead of accepting the first draft.

The status-quo-bias aside (p. 49) is usually read as a warning that people
resist new tools. Read forward instead: the bias that usually slows adoption
of a new technology for a generation got overrun in a couple of years here,
which is itself evidence that when a tool is genuinely useful and cheap
enough to just try, the normal drag on adoption doesn't hold the way it used
to. That's good news specifically for anyone waiting for permission before
they experiment.

## The invitation to the group

Pick one real decision this week, not a toy prompt, and deliberately assign
the AI a persona built to disagree with your instinct: the skeptical
colleague, the outside expert, the person with nothing to lose by telling
you the hard thing. Then bring back what it caught that you wouldn't have
caught alone. If p. 50's claim is right, that AI's strengths and weaknesses
don't mirror your own, this is the cheapest way anyone in the group could
test it this week, and the one experiment here worth actually regretting if
nobody runs it.
