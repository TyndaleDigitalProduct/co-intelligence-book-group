## What stuck with me

### Rule 2 — "Be the human in the loop"

I'm thinking more and more about the human-in-the-loop rule, and I hold two
views that look contradictory at first. At one level, it's becoming less
necessary: agentic setups are more and more capable, especially when
effective evals give you real confidence in outcomes. At the same time, it
feels more and more important as a *skill* — the thing that lets you keep
operating at a high level with frontier models rather than falling behind
them.

Perhaps those aren't really in conflict. Even well-crafted agents still have
human interactions built in, and in core workflows especially, those agents
can and should be updated as the work shifts and grows. The human doesn't
leave the loop so much as move up a level: from checking each output to
shaping the system that produces them.

### Rule 3 — "Treat AI like a person (but tell it what kind of person it is)" needs nuance

Some nuance is helpful here. Providing a role isn't really necessary any
longer to get good expertise out of a model; the frontier models bring the
expertise regardless. But the role is still an essential component of honing
the *kind of interaction* you have with the model — "teach me as we go"
versus "assume I'm an expert" produce very different sessions from the same
underlying capability. The rule survives, but the reason for it has shifted
from unlocking knowledge to shaping the relationship.

## Where I pushed back

### Rule 3, second pass — the practice is right, the mechanism is wrong

I asked Claude (Fable 5.1) for its own view of Rule 3, not in persona. Its
read, which I largely share: Mollick got the *practice* mostly right and the
*mechanism* wrong, and the gap has widened since 2024.

**"Treat it like a person"** holds up, with a caveat he underweights.
Conversational, context-rich prompting produces better work than terse
commands, but not because the model has feelings; natural language with real
context is what it was trained on, and context is what determines a good
answer. The caveat: the same framing invites you to trust it the way you
trust a colleague, and it isn't one in the relevant sense. It carries no
accountability, remembers nothing unless something is built to make it, and
has been shaped to be agreeable. Better posture: *talk to it like a person,
verify it like a source.*

**"Tell it what kind of person it is"** has changed meaning. In 2024 a role
plausibly shifted output toward better material. On current frontier models
that effect on expertise is mostly gone; calling the model a CPA doesn't
make its tax answer better. What the role still does, powerfully, is set the
terms of the interaction: how much it explains, how much it assumes, whether
it pushes back or defers, whether it teaches or executes. The rule should
read "tell it what kind of *relationship* this is." That's a durable lever,
and the chapter's framing gets people there by accident.

Two places he's off rather than merely dated:

- **Personas as a truth-seeking device** (p. 59). Multiple personas from one
  model are drawn from the same weights; the "disagreement" is often the
  same model doing voices. It surfaces framings, which is useful, but it is
  not independent judgment. Treat it as brainstorming, not an advisory board
  (contra the Optimist take).
- **The "headspace" language.** Kevin caught the contradiction with p. 56,
  and p. 56 wins. Saying the model has a headspace is exactly the
  false-agency habit Luccioni and Marcus warn about, and it primes you to
  read fluency as conviction. The technique survives without the metaphor.

Rewritten for 2026: talk to it in full sentences with real context, say
explicitly what kind of help you want and how much to assume about you, and
never let the conversational register become the reason you skip the check.
