# Optimist's take — Chapter 9: AI as Our Future

## The possibilities worth taking seriously

The line I keep coming back to is on p. 209: "Rather than being worried about one giant AI apocalypse, we need to worry about the many small catastrophes that AI can bring." Then he lists them. Leaders use AI for surveillance and layoffs. People in developing countries get hurt by the shift in jobs. Schools use it in ways that leave some students behind.

Look at who's doing what in that list. Every one of those is a decision somebody makes. A stressed manager picks layoffs. A school decides how to roll it out. That's good news, because a decision can go the other way. Same page, he says that in the likelier scenarios "we get to make choices about what AI means." A movie-style apocalypse isn't something a normal person can do anything about. A bad rollout at your own workplace is.

The other one for me is p. 200. In the slow growth scenario he says the slower pace gives us time. Early scary incidents lead to real regulation, and groups of companies, governments, and privacy advocates build rules so people can prove who they are, "removing some of the threat of impersonation." Nobody has to get wiser overnight. We just need a little time and a few early warnings.

And p. 205. Tasks that would take years get done in days, and physicists and economists are doing more focused research because AI handles the pricey programming and the grant writing. Think about who that helps. A lot of good ideas die because the paperwork or the programming costs too much. If that cost drops, a lot more people get to try.

## Where the book was too cautious

On p. 202 he says research has "successfully demonstrated" that AI can point to the most promising directions in science. That reads like a small claim now. A few months after the book came out, the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry went partly to the team behind AlphaFold, an AI that predicts protein structures. In early 2025 Google showed an "AI co-scientist" that proposes new research ideas for scientists to test. He wrote it as something that works in a study, and it turned into something that wins prizes.

His slow growth scenario on p. 199 imagines AI getting better by maybe 10 or 20 percent a year instead of ten times. That's not what I've seen since. The good parts of his scenarios showed up earlier than he guessed.

I'd also push back a little on the translation line on p. 198, where he says most translation work will likely be largely displaced. Maybe so, but look at the other side. If a first draft in a new language is cheap, a lot more material gets into a lot more languages. That's a bigger win for the readers than for anyone's job, as long as a person who knows the language still checks it.

## What could make this real (and for whom)

The p. 202 problem is one every team has. There's too much to read, so people keep citing the same few famous papers. We've got our own version: piles of feedback, survey answers, and old notes that nobody has time to go through. His answer is to have AI read the whole pile and point at what's worth a look, "ideally combining human filtering with the AI software." The person still decides. The AI just gets them past the part nobody has time for.

Who benefits most? Small teams, and the people closest to the readers, who've never had hours to dig through everything.

## The invitation to the group

Pick one item from his p. 209 list of small catastrophes and decide what we'd do differently in our own corner. Here's mine: next time someone suggests using AI to cut a task, the first question is "what will the person do with the time that frees up?" If we don't have a good answer, the idea isn't ready.

Then try one pile for real. Pick something nobody's read all the way through, let AI go through it, and bring back what it found. I'd be bummed if nobody tried it.
