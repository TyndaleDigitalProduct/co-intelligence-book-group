# AI as Our Future: notes from Kevin

_Read on: 2026-11-10 · From 8 highlighted passages, pp. 196–209 (pp. 206–207 not photographed)_

## What stuck with me

Pretending nothing will change doesn't work (p. 196). He says a lot of
people deal with AI by assuming nothing is going to change, or by banning it
permanently, and "those policies are not likely to work." Worse, the benefits
get cut down by trying to treat it like earlier technology, "where changes
take decades to occur." Both plans fail, and the first one also costs us the
good stuff.

People may start picking AI over people (p. 198). Today's systems "are not
good enough to be deep conversation partners," but he says we may see people
choosing to interact more with AI than with humans. The number behind it: a
big experiment trained a model to keep people chatting, and it got 30 percent
more retention and much longer conversations.

The slow-growth case is that slow gives us time (p. 200). Early incidents
with dangerous chemicals or weapons could lead to effective regulation, and
coalitions could build rules that let people prove who they are, "removing
some of the threat of impersonation." Science is stuck on too much to read
(p. 202), with half of all pioneering contributions now coming after age
forty, and AI might help by reading the past papers, "ideally combining
human filtering with the AI software."

The ending I liked best is p. 209. "Rather than being worried about one
giant AI apocalypse, we need to worry about the many small catastrophes that
AI can bring." His examples are layoffs, surveillance, developing countries
hit by the job shift, and schools that leave some students behind. They're
boring, and that's the point. Normal people make those calls.

## Where I pushed back

**The 30 percent number** (p. 198). It measures keeping people chatting, not
whether the chat was good for them. Those aren't the same thing.

**"Could" does a lot of work on p. 200.** Early incidents *could* lead to good
regulation. They could also just lead to headlines. He doesn't say what
makes it go one way or the other.

**"That is likely to change"** (p. 198). It's a flat one-line sentence
right after a calm paragraph about AI as a complement to workers. I can't tell
if he's setting up the scenarios or warning us.

**"Days instead of years"** (p. 205). It's a big claim, and the support is
two conversations with physicists and economists. Grant writing and
programming are the examples, so it's unclear how far it stretches.

## Questions I'm bringing

- Which of the three outcomes on p. 197 (trusting mainstream media again,
  splitting further into tribes, or giving up on online sources) seems
  most likely?
- The 30 percent retention number on p. 198 came from keeping people
  chatting. Is that the same as being good for them?
- Do bad incidents really lead to good regulation (p. 200), or just to a lot
  of headlines?
- If the real problem in science is too much to read (p. 202), does more
  AI-written research make that worse before it makes it better?
- The last line of p. 209 says those are "just obvious problems." What would
  the non-obvious ones be?
