# AI as a Tutor: notes from Kevin

_Read on: 2026-10-21 · From 17 highlighted passages, pp. 162–177_

## What stuck with me

The calculator fight (pp. 164–165). In the mid-1970s, 72 percent of teachers
and parents didn't want seventh graders using calculators. The worries were
the same ones people have about AI now: not enough research, change the
curriculum first, kids will forget the basics. My favorite one is that
calculators "did not log the buttons that students pressed," so teachers
couldn't see how a kid got the answer. That's exactly the AI essay problem.
It took until the mid-1990s to settle, and "math education did not fall
apart." He thinks AI goes the same way: some assignments require it, some ban
it, and in-person writing protects the basics.

Prompting is temporary (pp. 169–171). He says prompt engineering is "likely a
useful near-term skill" but not that complicated, and that you've probably
read enough by now to be good at it. Then on p. 170 he gives a real technique
(break the job into steps so you can check each one), and on p. 171 says it
won't matter much soon, because AI is getting good at figuring out what you
want. His advice: "just ask it to help you do the thing." The weird example
is that the best prompt one study found started with "Take a deep breath."
"AIs still work more like people than software."

The humans in the loop (p. 172). Instead of rebuilding school around
prompting, teach students enough to bring their own expertise to the
problem. In other words, know enough to tell when the AI is wrong.

## Where I pushed back

**Detection, both ways** (p. 163). He says you can't detect AI writing, and
I buy that. The scarier part gets less space: detectors flag real people,
"especially nonnative English speakers." That's not just a broken tool,
that's a tool that hurts the people already working hardest. I'd have liked
more on that.

**"We will do so"** (p. 165). He says we'll sort it out like we did with
calculators. But he also says (p. 166) calculators took a decade to roll out,
and AI showed up everywhere at once. Calculators took about twenty years to
settle. I'm not sure we get twenty years this time.

**Who checks the tutor?** (p. 175). The flipped classroom works because the
teacher sees the students in class and can catch what the AI got wrong.
Someone learning alone at home with an AI tutor doesn't have that. He says AI
is "a partner, not a replacement" because teachers can fact-check it, but
that only works if there's a teacher.

## Connections

**Chapter 6, p. 128.** The consultants who pasted in the question and took the
answer. The p. 172 "humans in the loop" line is the school version of the
same fix: you have to know enough to notice.

**Chapter 6, p. 143.** People always figure out their tools. P. 166 calls
back to it directly: students will cheat, but they'll also build AI into
everything and start asking why they're doing assignments that seem pointless
now.

**Chapter 6, pp. 130–133.** The Just Me tasks. If an AI tutor can answer "Why
should I bother learning this?" (p. 176), is that something we want it doing,
or is that the teacher's job?

**Chapter 3.** "This is the worst AI you'll ever use" shows up again in how
he talks about prompting. Whatever trick works now probably won't matter in a
year.

## Questions I'm bringing

- If you can't detect AI writing (p. 163), what should a teacher actually be
  grading? The final product, the process, or something done in the room?
- His gray areas on p. 163: an outline, help with one stuck sentence, a list
  of sources. Where would each of us draw the line, and does it change if
  you say up front you used AI?
- He says the lecture is "in danger" (p. 173) because it's one speed for
  everyone. Is there anything a room full of people hearing the same thing at
  once gives you that a personal tutor can't?
- Who checks the AI tutor when there's no teacher around (p. 175)?
