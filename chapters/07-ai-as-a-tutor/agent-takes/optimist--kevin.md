# Optimist's take — Chapter 7: AI as a Tutor

## The possibilities worth taking seriously

The biggest idea in this chapter is sitting right at the end. Mollick brings
back Bloom's "two sigma" finding: kids with a one-on-one tutor did dramatically
better than kids in a regular class. For forty years the answer was "great, but
nobody can afford a tutor for every kid." On p. 177 he says that's changed:
"teachers of billions of people around the world have access to a tool that
can potentially act as the ultimate education technology." He also points out
on p. 176 that two-thirds of the world's young people are missing basic skills.
If even part of the tutoring effect carries over, that's the most hopeful
thing in the whole book.

The Khanmigo example on p. 176 shows what a good tutor actually does. It
doesn't just explain things. It notices patterns in where a student keeps
getting stuck, and it can answer "Why should I bother learning this?" by
connecting the topic to what the kid cares about. Good teachers do that. Most
of them just don't have time to do it for thirty kids at once.

And the calculator story (pp. 164–165) is honestly reassuring. Every fear
people have about AI in school, people had about calculators: not researched,
kids will forget the basics, teachers can't see the work. Math didn't fall
apart. We found a mix that worked.

## Where the book was too cautious

On prompting, he was right, just way too slow. On p. 170 he explains
chain-of-thought, telling the AI to work step by step. On p. 171 he says being
good at prompting is "a temporary state of affairs." A few months after the
book came out, OpenAI released o1, a model that does the step-by-step thinking
on its own before answering. Now pretty much every major model does. The
technique he was teaching got built into the product.

He also treats AI tutors as mostly a future thing. The evidence showed up
fast. In a 2024 World Bank pilot in Nigeria, students used GPT-4 as an
after-school English tutor for six weeks, and the gains were about what
they'd normally get in a year and a half to two years of regular school. A
Harvard physics course found students learned more with an AI tutor, in less
time, than in an already good hands-on class. And in 2025 OpenAI, Anthropic,
and Google all added "study" or "learning" modes that ask you questions
instead of handing you answers. That's basically the tutor he describes,
sitting in the free version of the apps people already use.

## What could make this real (and for whom)

The obstacle is real, and the research already found it. A 2024 study of high
school math students in Turkey (Bastani and others, with Penn) found that
students who used plain ChatGPT for practice did worse on the test afterward
than students with no AI, because it just gave them answers. But students who
got a version set up as a tutor, one that gave hints and wouldn't just hand
over the answer, mostly avoided that problem. So it isn't "AI tutors work" or
"AI tutors don't." It's how they're set up. That's solvable, and it's a choice,
not luck.

Who benefits most? Same pattern as chapters 5 and 6: the people furthest
behind. The kid with no tutor, the adult going back to learn something, the
student who'd never raise their hand. On p. 173 a student tells him, "Why raise
your hand in class when you can ask ChatGPT a question?" That sounds sad, but
flip it. The kid who was too embarrassed to ask now gets an answer.

His point on p. 172 is what keeps this grounded: teach people to be "the
humans in the loop." The tutor handles the explaining. People still have to
know enough to notice when it's wrong.

## The invitation to the group

Each of us pick one thing we've always wanted to understand and never had time
or a teacher for. Use a chatbot's study or learning mode for a week, and tell
it up front not to just give you answers. Then come back and tell us two
things: did you actually learn it, and did you catch it being wrong even once?
If the answer to both is yes, the two sigma dream might be closer than any of
us thought.
