# Chapter 2: Aligning the Alien — notes from Kevin

_Read on: 2026-08-25 · From ten highlighted passages, pp. 33–45_

I read the ten twice, once as somebody who uses these tools every day and
once as somebody who works at a publisher. The two readings don't agree, and
the disagreement is the part I most want to put in front of the group.

## What stuck with me

**Legality is a venue, not a standard** (p. 33). The same act is restricted
in Brussels, litigable in Washington, explicitly permitted in Tokyo, so the
murkiness isn't a stage waiting on a ruling, and conceding the legal argument
costs him nothing. The real grievance is his next line: most companies don't
ask. Fair use answers the complaint that a copy was made and says nothing
about a decision made about your work without you.

**The safety layer is thin structurally, not accidentally** (pp. 38, 40).
RLHF doesn't give the model morality, it constrains its ability to act on the
absence of one. So the pirate script isn't a hole in a filter, it's a conflict
between two objectives: don't explain napalm, and help this person. Reframe
until the second wins. And the follow-ups get easier once you're down that
path, so the vulnerability is the conversation, not the prompt.

**Bias arrives as a competence failure** (p. 36). The lawyer-and-assistant
test contains no slur, just accuracy dropping with the gender of the
professional. Fine-tuning trains what not to say, so it misses this, and the
error is silent: a wrong answer about who needed help looks exactly like a
right one.

## Where I pushed back

**"AI is a tool" (p. 42) is a claim, not an observation.** A hammer doesn't
need to be aligned. He reaches for the tool frame exactly when he needs a
social fix accepted instead of a technical one. Keep both: alien for
predicting what it will do, tool for deciding who answers for it.

**The diagnosis has mechanisms, the cure has adjectives** (pp. 43–45). He is
specific about why regulation will lag, then offers agreed-upon norms and an
inclusive process, which is the vocabulary of a problem nobody owns. Follow
the burden: companies won't, governments will lag, so the residual holder is
an informed public. That's a lot of weight on the least coordinated party.

**"Homage" (p. 34) claims an intent that isn't present**, and a work repeated
often enough comes back nearly verbatim anyway. "Not a copy" is the average
case, not a guarantee.

## Two readings that don't agree

Four places where the same page issues opposite instructions depending on
which chair I'm sitting in.

**The debt** (p. 33). At my desk, using a tool built without permission is a
quiet compromise I make daily. From the house, permission is the principle
we'd defend in public. Nobody has had to say both of those out loud in the
same room yet.

**The reframe** (p. 40). Personally it's a technique: give it a role and a
stake and the work gets better. Institutionally it's a vulnerability: anyone
can frame a request and get behavior we never sanctioned. One mechanism, and
my productivity tip is the house's open door.

**Verification** (p. 43). At my desk the checking is a cost I'm tempted to
skip when the draft looks fine. From the house it's the product. My shortcut
is the institution's defect, which is why it can't rest on whoever happens to
be tired that afternoon.

**Where the value sits** (p. 34). What impresses me is fluent prose on
demand. What the house should least depend on is fluent prose, since that's
now the cheapest thing in the building. What dazzles me is what we're least
able to sell.

## Connections

The three bias sources on p. 35 land in three places. Toxicity is brand risk.
Coverage is a translation risk, so generated Spanish and Portuguese needs
native review, not proofreading. Selection is invisible: what a model never
saw surfaces as an absence, and only editorial judgment catches those. Read
that way, pp. 35 and 36 are an argument for editors rather than against them.

Frequency (p. 34) inverts which assets matter. The most quoted content is the
most reproducible and least defensible; the backlist and the apparatus are
the reverse.

Two updates, both needing verification. The EU's text and data mining
exception works by rights reservation, so even the strictest regime presumes
permission unless you opt out machine-readably. And in *Bartz v. Anthropic*
the training was fair use while the pirated library was not. The training was
fine; the acquisition was the liability.

Also: p. 40 is prompt injection in plain English, and p. 45 makes help
articles and support replies the mechanism rather than adjacent to it.

## Questions I'm bringing

- Does Tyndale have a stated position on its content being used for AI
  training, and is anything reserved in a machine-readable way?
- If a submitted manuscript contains generated material, whose disclosure
  problem is that?
- Publishing has trade bodies and collective-licensing precedent. Is
  "coordination among companies" reachable in our sector where it isn't in
  most?
