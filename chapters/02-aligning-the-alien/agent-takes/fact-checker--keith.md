# Fact-check — Aligning the Alien

**Scope note:** run at Keith's request on one slice of the chapter — its
claims about *alignment governance*: how the US government, US frontier labs,
and Chinese frontier labs would approach regulation. Checked to August 2026.
The chapter's bias and jailbreak claims are left for another pass.

## Scoreboard

| Claim | Verdict | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| "Government regulation is likely to continue to lag the actual development of AI capabilities" (p. 44) | **HELD UP** | The first *binding* US frontier-AI law, [California's SB 53](https://fpf.org/blog/californias-sb-53-the-first-frontier-ai-law-explained/), was signed Sept 2025 and effective Jan 2026 — five-plus model generations after the book. Federal action came later still: the [June 2026 executive order](https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2026/06/new-ai-executive-order) is explicitly *voluntary* (30-day pre-release review, no licensing). |
| "…and might stifle positive innovation in an attempt to stop negative outcomes" (p. 44) | **CONTESTED** | The live fight is the inverse: the [July 2025 federal AI Action Plan](https://www.goodwinlaw.com/en/insights/publications/2025/11/alerts-technology-aiml-california-moves-to-regulate-frontier-ai-with-a-focus-on-catastrophic-risk) pressures *states* not to regulate, while Commerce's [forced suspension of foreign access to two frontier models](https://iapp.org/news/a/thought-for-the-week-us-government-order-forces-commercial-suspension-of-two-fronteir-ai-models) (June 2026) shows coercive power being used for *national security*, not alignment. Stifling-vs-safety is now an inter-governmental fight, not a government-vs-industry one. |
| Alignment in practice depends on labs self-policing (RLHF, red-teaming; pp. 38–41) | **HELD UP, then formalized** | Self-governance hardened into published frameworks: [Anthropic's RSP is at v3.x](https://www.anthropic.com/responsible-scaling-policy) with public risk reports, [OpenAI's Preparedness Framework v2](https://openai.com/index/updating-our-preparedness-framework/) (Apr 2025), DeepMind FSF v3 — see [METR's comparison](https://metr.org/fsp). Still voluntary and revocable: OpenAI [reportedly disbanded its preparedness team](https://www.itechpost.com/articles/237038/20260817/openai-reportedly-disbands-preparedness-team-ai-safety-strategy-shifts.htm) in Aug 2026 (unconfirmed by OpenAI at this writing). SB 53 now makes publishing such frameworks *mandatory* for the largest developers — the book's voluntary world is ending at the state level. |
| The two poles: Yudkowsky's shutdown vs. Altman's "boundless upside" (p. 31) | **SUPERSEDED** | Neither pole set policy. No jurisdiction paused; none deregulated fully. What emerged everywhere is a middle bureaucratic layer — transparency reports, incident reporting, compute thresholds (SB 53's 10^26 FLOP line) — that neither pole predicted. |
| Implicit frame: alignment governance is a Western/lab story (China absent from pp. 38–44) | **SUPERSEDED** | China regulated *earlier and harder* than the US, but aimed at content control, not alignment: binding [generative-AI Interim Measures](https://www.fasken.com/en/knowledge/2023/08/chinas-new-rules-for-generative-ai) since Aug 2023, AI-content labeling rules (2025), an AI-referencing Cybersecurity Law amendment (Jan 2026), and a [draft AI Law with a "Negative List"](https://asiasociety.org/policy-institute/chinas-emerging-approach-regulating-general-purpose-artificial-intelligence-balancing-innovation-and) requiring permission for some frontier models. [DeepSeek now runs frontier-risk evals but does not publish them](https://www.yahoo.com/news/articles/deepseek-evaluates-ai-models-frontier-093000763.html) — Beijing wants risk awareness without Western-style disclosure. |

## The most consequential update since 2024

The three-track divergence itself. Mollick wrote as if "regulation" were one
thing that lags. What actually happened: **US labs** built elaborate voluntary
frameworks (RSPs, preparedness levels) that are becoming quasi-mandatory via
California; the **US federal government** chose speed — voluntary review plus
export-control muscle, while actively suppressing state regulation; and
**China** built the world's most binding AI rules while defining "alignment"
as alignment *with state values and social stability* — its labs evaluate
frontier risk privately and say nothing. Three governance cultures, three
different answers to "aligned to what?"

## What this does to the chapter's argument

The p. 44 prediction is the chapter's best-aged sentence, but it undersells
the twist: regulation didn't just lag — it *fragmented*. That strengthens
Mollick's deeper claim that alignment can't be left to any single actor, and
sharpens the group's standing question (see Keith's notes): every regime that
has emerged answers "who decides?" differently — a lab's safety team, a
state legislature, a security agency, or the Party. None of them answers it
with broad societal engagement, which is what Mollick actually called for.
