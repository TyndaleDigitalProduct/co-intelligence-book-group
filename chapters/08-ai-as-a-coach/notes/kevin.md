# AI as a Coach: notes from Kevin

_Read on: 2026-10-27 · From 14 highlighted passages, pp. 178–192_

## What stuck with me

The real danger isn't homework (p. 178). He opens by saying the biggest
threat AI poses to education is to the "hidden system of apprenticeship"
that comes after school, not to essays. Nobody designed that system, but
it's how people actually learn to do real work. The reason it breaks is
honest: a boss would usually "rather just see the job done fast" than deal
with a new person's mistakes and feelings (p. 179), so they just use AI.
Nobody decides to stop training people. It just stops being worth the
hassle.

The surgery story (pp. 179–180). With robotic surgery only one person runs
the machine, usually the senior surgeon, so residents mostly watch. The ones
who wanted to learn did "shadow learning" on YouTube or practiced on live
patients more than they should have. His point is that this spreads to
every field as AI takes over the beginner tasks, right when we most need
experts to check the AI.

Deliberate practice (pp. 183–184). It's not hours ("10,000 hours is not a
magical threshold"), it's harder reps plus real feedback from a coach. The
piano example sums it up: Sophie plays what she's already comfortable with,
Naomi works with an instructor on harder and harder pieces. Same hours,
Naomi gets better. Good coaches are rare, and that's where he thinks AI can
help.

## Where I pushed back

**Raj isn't real** (pp. 184–185). The architect who gets instant AI feedback
after every design and outgrows his weekly-feedback classmate is a made-up
example, not a study. And on p. 186 he admits today's AI "cannot achieve
this entire vision." The real thing they built, a pitching simulator, took
four separate AIs to work around the lack of memory.

**Fast feedback isn't the same as good feedback.** A coach who's always
there but sometimes wrong (or just flattering) might be worse than a human
who shows up once a week. He doesn't really deal with that.

**"Need not lead to replacement"** (p. 190). "Need not" is doing a lot of
work. The Turnitin CEO on p. 189 is saying he'll need 20 percent of his
engineers. Getting better at a few tasks *can* lead to replacement, and he
doesn't say what decides which way it goes.

**Picking a lane in school** (p. 191). Narrow focus starting as a student
feels early. How do you know what your lane is at 16?

## Connections

**Chapter 7.** Last chapter was all about cheating on homework. This one
says that's the smaller problem, which is a nice turn.

**Chapter 6, p. 136.** Cyborgs and Centaurs come back on p. 182: the more AI
augments our work, the more we need human expertise to check it.

**Chapter 6, p. 156.** AI gives the biggest boost to the weakest workers.
Same finding here as "it levels the playing field" (p. 188), backed by the
BCG gap shrinking from 22 percent to 4 percent (p. 189). The odd detail: the
top law students actually got slightly worse with AI.

**Chapter 3 and chapter 7, p. 172.** "Be the human in the loop" keeps
coming back. Here it's the reason to keep learning the basics (p. 181): you
can't catch the AI's mistakes without facts in your own head.

## Questions I'm bringing

- If bosses skip training juniors because AI is faster (p. 179), where do
  the next experts come from? Whose job is it to fix that?
- What would Naomi-style deliberate practice (p. 183) look like for a
  normal job, where most of us repeat what we're already good at?
- Is an always-available AI coach that's sometimes wrong better or worse
  than a human coach once a week (pp. 184–186)?
- Why did the best law students get worse with AI (p. 189)?
- Which basics are actually worth memorizing now (p. 181), and which really
  can be looked up?
