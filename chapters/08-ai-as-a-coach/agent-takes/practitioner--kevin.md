# Practitioner's take: Chapter 8, AI as a Coach

Mollick's big worry in this chapter isn't cheating. It's that bosses will skip the intern because AI is faster (p. 179), and then nobody grows into the expert we need to check the AI (p. 180). His fix is deliberate practice: harder reps, real feedback, a coach pushing you (p. 183), with AI as the coach good humans are too busy to be (p. 184). So what does anyone actually do with that on Monday?

## Do these two things first

**1. Draft first, then ask for the critique.** When you start a task in a skill you want to keep sharp (a hard email, a report, a plan, a piece of code), do your own version before you open the AI. Then paste it in and ask for the three biggest problems, not a rewrite. That's the Naomi version of practice from p. 183: you do the hard rep, then get feedback on it. If you let the AI write first and you just edit, you're Sophie playing the songs she already knows.

*How you'll know it's working:* keep a running list of what the critique flags. After a month the same notes should show up less. If they don't, you're reading the feedback but not using it.

**2. When "faster to just do it with AI" crosses your mind, stop and check who's losing a rep.** That thought is the exact moment p. 179 describes. Sometimes doing it yourself is right. But if there's a newer person who'd learn from the task, give it to them with AI as their coach, and you review the result. Then spend five minutes explaining *why* you changed what you changed. That explanation is the part of the old apprenticeship AI can't replace yet.

*How you'll know it's working:* in three months, can that person handle the task without you checking every line?

## The longer list

- **Name your lane.** Mollick says pick a narrow area to go deep on (p. 191). Write one sentence: "The thing I want to be the human in the loop for is ___." Use it to decide which tasks get practice #1 and which just get handed off.
- **Keep your facts in your head.** The paradox on p. 181 is that you still need the basics stored up to think with. That means the core facts of whatever field you're in. If you have to look up something you get asked about every week, learn it.
- **Turn up the difficulty.** Ask the AI to play a hard version of whoever you're actually up against: a skeptical boss, a tough client, a picky reviewer, an exam grader. Make it harder each round. That's the "ratcheting up" part people skip.

**Updated since 2024:** Mollick's pitch simulator needed four separate AIs because the models had no memory (p. 186). That's mostly gone. Current tools remember past chats, have built-in study or learning modes, and handle voice role-play in one conversation. You don't need a Wharton setup anymore. One well-written prompt gets you most of the way there. The worry on p. 179 has aged too, just the other way. A 2025 Stanford study (Brynjolfsson and colleagues, "Canaries in the Coal Mine") found early-career jobs shrinking in the most AI-exposed fields while experienced workers held steady. His training gap is starting to show up in real hiring data.

## Risks and guardrails

- **A coach that only cheers.** AI feedback tends to flatter. Tell it plainly: "Be blunt, assume this is not ready to send." And if it says everything's great, push back once.
- **Fast doesn't mean right.** Raj's always-on mentor (p. 184) is made up, and Mollick admits AI still hallucinates (p. 186). Check any factual correction against the source before you take it.
- **Getting rusty.** If AI does all of a task for months, you lose the ability to catch its mistakes, which is chapter 6's falling-asleep-at-the-wheel problem. Practice #1 is the guardrail. Do the real rep on the stuff you're supposed to be the expert on.
- **Confidential stuff.** Don't paste other people's personal details or anything private from work into a personal AI account. Strip the details or use approved tools.

## Proposed group experiment

**One skill, three reps.** Everyone picks one skill in their lane, work or not. Do it three times before the next meeting using practice #1: your draft first, then a blunt AI critique, then revise. Log in `experiments/` what the critique caught on rep one versus rep three, and whether you'd trust its notes. At the meeting we compare: did anyone actually get better, or just get better edits?
